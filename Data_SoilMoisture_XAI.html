Exploring the Impact of Meteorological Variables on Soil Moisture Using Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)

Predicting soil moisture with machine learning and using SHAP to understand the contributions of precipitation and temperature.

Read the full report ↓

01 / Project Overview

Predicting soil moisture and explaining why

In this 2024 project, I used ERA5 monthly climate data from 1950–2023 to model soil moisture across ten agricultural regions in China. I prepared the data, built temporal features, trained Random Forest, XGBoost and LSTM models, and used SHAP to interpret how weather conditions contributed to their predictions.

02 / Methods & Findings

How I applied machine learning

Data preparation and temporal validation

I used 888 months of ERA5 data (1950–2023), with total precipitation and 2 m air temperature as predictors. I combined the first three soil water layers using depth weights to represent moisture in the upper metre. The study covered ten agricultural regions in mainland China, each containing multiple 0.25° grid cells.

Within each grid cell, I assigned the earliest 80% of records to training and the latest 20% to validation, preserving time order. I pooled grid-cell records within each region and trained one model per algorithm per region: 30 models in total.

Comparing three model configurations

All models predicted current-month soil moisture from precipitation and temperature histories. I constructed lagged inputs for the tree models and sequential inputs for LSTM.

Model performance across ten regions

I compared held-out R² scores by region. LSTM performed best in nine of the ten regions; XGBoost was slightly ahead in region 8. Lower scores in some northern and western regions showed where precipitation and temperature alone captured less of the soil moisture variation.

What SHAP revealed

I used TreeExplainer for Random Forest and XGBoost, and GradientExplainer for LSTM, to inspect the contribution of each weather variable and month. Precipitation generally contributed more than temperature, and previous-month precipitation often ranked first. Higher precipitation generally pushed predictions upward, while higher temperature pushed them downward.

P = precipitation; T = temperature; 0 = current month; −1 = previous month. Pink indicates higher feature values and blue lower values. Positive SHAP values increase a prediction relative to its reference value; negative values decrease it.

Limits of interpretation: regional results varied, and explanations were not always consistent between models. The tree-model SHAP analysis used the full datasets, while LSTM used held-out data only. These differences limit direct comparison. SHAP explains model behaviour and does not establish causation. Future work would add in-situ observations and variables such as vegetation and groundwater.
